## Introduction {#sec:intro}

A learner compounds experience when solving earlier problems makes later, related problems cheaper to solve. Learning to learn has a natural in-context form in language models: if a prompt holds several complete instances of a structured task, the model may do better on the next instance than it would without them. Such improvement is often read as evidence that the model has abstracted the shared structure. The reading matters, because an abstraction transfers to instances that share the structure but none of the surface, whereas reuse of the examples does not.

Behavioural improvement alone cannot establish abstraction, because at least three other explanations produce it. First, every added example lengthens the context, and context length by itself changes performance. Second, if an earlier example contains the answer, a model can recall or copy it without any inference. Third, a model can match the new instance against the stored examples and answer by analogy to the closest one. Exemplar matching improves with more examples just as abstraction does, and the two differ only on types that the history does not contain. In information-theoretic terms, a learner that stores the examples verbatim and one that has extracted their rules hold the same information about the task; they differ only in what that information makes cheaply usable [@xu2020usable]. A test of compounding therefore has to control context length, separate recall from inference, and include held-out types.

Algebraic operation tables make such a test exact. A partial Cayley table, with some cells revealed and the rest queried, determines some of its missing cells under weak assumptions and others only under strong ones. Whether a queried cell is fixed by the Latin property alone, by the loop axioms (identity and cancellation), or only through associativity can be decided exactly, by enumeration or SAT, for every cell of every trial. Each cell thus receives a *forcing class*, which states what a learner must know to answer it. Writing each table in fresh, arbitrary word labels removes semantic cues, and the earlier tables can be manipulated directly: replaced by length-matched tables without group structure, restricted to isomorphism types other than the target's, or made to contain the target's own labelled table. Exact idealised learners can then be run on the very trials the models see.

The closest work is In-Context Algebra [@todd2026ica]. Todd et al. train small transformers on sequences of group facts, with symbols reassigned in every sequence, and identify the networks' mechanisms: commutative copying, identity recognition and closure-based cancellation, with associative composition the weakest route. They do not measure how accuracy depends on the number of earlier tables, label cells by forcing class, compare against an exemplar baseline at equal budget, or evaluate pretrained language models. A second line of work asks whether in-context learners memorise or generalise. Raventós et al. [@raventos2023] find a task-diversity threshold below which a transformer behaves like a Bayesian predictor over the discrete set of its pretraining tasks, and Wurgaft et al. [@wurgaft2025] cast the trade-off between such memorising and generalising predictors in a hierarchical Bayesian account. Our exemplar and abstraction learners are in-context analogues of these two predictors, with the "seen tasks" supplied in the prompt under fresh labels rather than in pretraining. Chan et al. [@chan2022] report that transformers generalise from context mainly through exemplars and from weights mainly through rules, while pretrained language models generalise from context in a more rule-based way; held-out types test this contrast directly. Under the view of in-context learning as implicit Bayesian inference [@xie2021], a learner that already holds the true environment prior gains nothing from earlier tables written in fresh labels, and our Bayesian baselines make that prediction exact. Matsutani et al. [@matsutani2026] report gains from relabelled demonstrations of other worlds in Qwen3 base models, but with one shared vocabulary and, as far as we found, no length-matched control. We know of no published evaluation of pretrained language models on in-context group-table completion with fresh labels.

We turn this design into a *compounding fingerprint*. A model sees a **history** of $t = 0$, 1, 2 or 4 complete earlier tables, each written in its own fresh single-token English words, followed by a target table of order $n = 5$, 6 or 8 (a group, or in one $n = 5$ cell a loop) that reveals $k$ of its cells, the **cue**. Every unrevealed cell is queried and scored by forced choice, and every cell carries its forcing class: Latin-forced (L), loop-forced (O), associativity-forced (G), forced only by commutativity or cyclicity (Ab, Cy), or undetermined (U). Base histories are compared with a length-matched **scramble control**, **held-out types**, a **leak control** that contains the target's own labelled table, and non-associative loops, over 1,800 trials per model. A preregistered decision rule maps the resulting signature to the learner classes R, N, N_iso, A and B-type. We applied it to seven exact idealised learners, to nine checkpoints (ten model–precision configurations) from three families [@qwen3; @smollm2; @olmo2], to seven small transformers trained from scratch, and to the released In-Context Algebra model. We used the same stimuli to test an activation-space direction from our own earlier preprint, the compute–retrieve (CR) axis [@ramdan2026cr]. The three largest pretrained models ran in 4-bit nf4 quantisation, whose preregistered control failed its tolerance. All model inference and training ran on one 8 GB laptop GPU, and every deviation from preregistration is listed in @app:deviations.

Our contributions are:

- **An exact, preregistered measurement.** On identical trials, the decision rule returns the correct class for all seven idealised learners at $n = 6$ and $n = 8$, with 0 misclassifications. At $n = 6$, abstraction and exemplar matching both gain about +0.70 over scramble on G cells, and only held-out transfer separates them cleanly: +0.70 for abstraction against +0.03 for exemplar matching (@sec:r-ideal).
- **A calibrated null for pretrained models up to 8B.** All ten configurations are class R. None compounds: four history tables minus the length-matched scramble lies between −0.019 and +0.026, and every 95% CI includes 0. None infers Latin-forced cells in direct-answer mode; in the eight models swept over cue density, this holds even at dense cues where one-pass row/column elimination would score 0.92 (post hoc). Recall is family-dependent: Qwen3 recalls a previously seen labelled table at 0.95–0.96 at 4–8B, OLMo-2 at 0.31–0.37 (@sec:r-llm; @sec:r-cal).
- **A count baseline.** Small transformers trained from scratch on group, Latin and loop diets learn a global count shortcut: they answer with the symbol least represented in the cue. The post-hoc count learner C matches five of the six learned networks to within 0.001–0.007. On the in-training evaluation the shortcut alone scores 0.33 at $n = 6$, enough to pass the preregistered learning-failure threshold (@sec:r-trained).
- **A positive control on a real network.** The released In-Context Algebra model is classified non-R, non-B: at $t = 0$, Latin- and loop-forced cells score 0.89–0.99 and associativity-forced cells 0.53–0.70. In a post hoc split, it beats an exact loop-level baseline at $n = 6$ and $n = 8$ but not at $n = 5$, and it partly answers by commutative copying. Its class changes during training (exploratory). Its 16-token vocabulary leaves the compounding branch untested (@sec:r-ica).
- **A validity test for activation "mode" probes.** The CR axis separates its own calibration prompts with AUC 0.96–0.99, but on a format- and length-matched retrieval/inference contrast it moves significantly in the predicted direction (CI excluding 0) in 0 of 5 models and is significantly reversed in 3. In an exploratory control, context vocabulary explains 64–87% of the reversal; with vocabulary matched, a retrieval signal of the predicted sign appears in the post-trained model and without the chat template (@sec:r-cr).

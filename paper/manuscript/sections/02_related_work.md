## Related work {#sec:related}

#### In-context learning of algebraic structure

The closest prior work is *In-Context Algebra* [@todd2026ica]. Todd et al. train small transformers (4 layers, 8 heads, width 1024) on sequences of facts $xy = z$ from cyclic groups C3–C10 and dihedral groups D3–D5 (as the paper states; the released code for the checkpoint we score also samples D2 = Z2×Z2), with the map from group elements to tokens redrawn for every sequence. Their models reach near-perfect accuracy, transfer to held-out order-8 groups, and use commutative copying, identity recognition and closure-based cancellation; associative composition is the weakest route, at 60.2% on the sequences that test it, which our forcing classes turn into an exact per-cell label (associativity-forced cells, whose accuracy also credits commutative copying; @sec:r-ica). That work evaluates no pretrained language models and does not vary the number of earlier, differently labelled tables, of which the released model's 16-token vocabulary fits at most one. Adjacent work studies algebra learned in weights: grokking of finite-dimensional algebras [@notsawo2026grokking], and in-context modular arithmetic that generalises out of distribution only above a threshold number of pretraining tasks [@he2024grok]. "Cayley-table completion" in [@huh2025algebraic; @huh2026cayley] is a different problem, recovering a table by tensor factorisation rather than inferring it in context.

#### Memorising and generalising learners

Below a task-diversity threshold, transformers trained on linear regression act as a Bayesian estimator over the discrete set of pretraining tasks, and above it as ridge regression [@raventos2023]; Wurgaft et al. [@wurgaft2025] explain such transitions as a posterior-weighted trade-off between a memorising and a generalising predictor, of which our learners N_iso and A (@sec:learners) are in-context analogues. Chan et al. [@chan2022] report that transformers trained on controlled stimuli generalise from context mainly through exemplars, whereas pretrained language models do so in a more rule-based way, increasingly with scale. Held-out types test this contrast directly, and the shape of compounding (gradual for exemplar matching, a step at $t = 1$ for abstraction) would test it again had any pretrained checkpoint gained beyond the scramble control. If in-context learning is implicit Bayesian inference [@xie2021], a learner that already holds the true environment prior gains nothing from fresh-label histories; our Bayes learner B makes this exact.

#### Recall, mode probes and structure defined in context

Induction heads complete a repeated pattern AB…A with B [@olsson2022], and symbol-abstraction, symbolic-induction and retrieval heads together carry out abstract rule induction in language models [@yang2025symbolic]; our learner R corresponds to verbatim copying, which the leak control measures directly. Activation-space work reads task or processing mode from directions such as task vectors [@hendel2023], function vectors carried by a few attention heads [@todd2023fv], representations a model is poised to verbalise [@gurnee2026workspace] and the compute–retrieve axis of our earlier preprint [@ramdan2026cr], which we test on format- and length-matched (not vocabulary-matched) stimuli in @app:cr. Evidence on whether pretrained models adopt semantics defined only in the prompt is mixed: overriding flipped labels emerges with scale [@wei2023larger], counterfactual variants such as arithmetic in unfamiliar bases degrade performance [@wu2024reasoning], models underperform n-gram baselines on random formal languages [@gupta2025random], in-context learning in Pythia sits between memorisation and a symbolic algorithm [@niu2025illusion], and models reorganise their representations to match a graph defined in context [@park2024iclr] but often cannot use them downstream [@lepori2026]; representational change within a context suggests that in-context learning is not one process [@hosseini2026], and our results fit this picture.

#### Cross-episode gains and usable information

Matsutani et al. [@matsutani2026] come closest to compounding: few-shot demonstrations from different worlds improve in-context world modelling across several model families, but their worlds reassign one shared set of words to the graph's nodes, whereas our history tables use fresh words, and we found no length-matched control like our scramble history. Storing past tables verbatim and extracting their axioms yield the same Shannon information, but predictive V-information [@xu2020usable], which measures what a computationally bounded observer can use and which computation can create, frames our equal-budget comparison: N_iso and A enumerate at most the same number of candidate tables and differ only in what the history makes cheaply usable. For Latin squares, a critical set is a minimal set of entries that determines the square, and the smallest one corresponds to the minimum teaching dimension of the class [@hatami2016]; our forcing classes ask a cell-level version for one fixed cue: which unrevealed cells it already determines, and under which of the nested classes Latin, loop and group.

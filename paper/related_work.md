**In-context learning of algebraic structure.** The closest work on the task is *In-Context Algebra* [1]. Todd et al. train small transformers (4 layers, 8 heads, width 1024) on sequences of facts "x y = z" drawn from cyclic groups C3–C10 and dihedral groups D3–D5 (as stated in [1]; the released code for the checkpoint we score in Experiment 6 also samples D2 = Z2×Z2). The map from group elements to tokens is redrawn for every sequence, and the groups in one sequence use disjoint token sets. Models reach near-perfect accuracy, generalise to held-out order-8 groups, and implement three identifiable mechanisms: commutative copying, identity recognition, and closure-based cancellation. Associative composition is the weakest route (60.2% in their analysis). Our cell taxonomy turns this into an exact measurement. A queried cell is *O-forced* when identity and cancellation alone fix its value, which are the mechanisms Todd et al. find. It is *G-forced* when only associativity does. We know of no work that evaluates pretrained LLMs on this task, or that asks whether earlier, differently labelled tables in the same context improve completion of a later one; [1] does not do either. Adjacent lines study algebra learned in weights: grokking extended to finite-dimensional algebras [2]; from-scratch in-context modular arithmetic, which generalises out of distribution only above a threshold number of pretraining tasks [3]; and "Cayley-table completion" by tensor factorisation [4, 5]. That last use of the phrase is a different problem and needs a footnote.

**Memorising versus generalising in-context learners.** Raventós et al. [6] show a task-diversity threshold in transformers trained on linear regression. Below it, the model behaves like a Bayesian estimator whose prior is the discrete set of pretraining tasks. Above it, the model matches ridge regression, which uses a prior over all tasks. Wurgaft et al. [7] unify such findings in a hierarchical Bayesian account. A model trades a *memorising* predictor (discrete prior on seen tasks) against a *generalising* one (the true task prior), weighted by a posterior that moves during training and by a complexity penalty. Our N_iso (isomorphism matching against stored exemplar tables) and A (axiom induction) are in-context analogues of these two predictors. The difference is that in our setting the "seen tasks" are supplied in the prompt rather than in pretraining, under fresh labels. Chan et al. [8] report a related contrast. Transformers trained on controlled stimuli generalise from context mostly by exemplars and from weights mostly by rules, but pretrained language models generalise from context in a more rule-based way, and more so as they get larger. That prediction can be tested directly in our design: exemplar-like compounding should follow the missing-mass curve, and rule-like compounding should be a step.

**ICL as implicit Bayesian inference.** Xie et al. [9] explain ICL as inference of a latent document-level concept, proved for mixtures of HMMs. Under this view, a learner that already holds the true environment prior gains nothing from earlier tables that carry fresh labels: they are independent of the target given the environment. Our Bayes baselines (B, and the hierarchical Bh) make this exact, and they bound what an ideal learner can gain from history by the log prior weight of the environment.

**Task vectors, function vectors and induction heads.** Mechanistic work locates ICL in identifiable circuits. Induction heads complete [A][B]…[A]→[B] and coincide with a phase change in ICL ability [10]. Demonstrations are compressed into a single "task vector" [11] or "function vector" carried by a few attention heads [12]. Yang et al. [13] describe symbol-abstraction heads, symbolic induction heads and retrieval heads that together carry out abstract rule induction in LLMs. Our recall-only learner R corresponds to verbatim induction-style copying. The leak control in Experiment 1 confirms that small models can do this copying (accuracy 0.44–0.65 when the target's own labelled table is in context), while they fail when inference is required. Whether compounding, where it appears, travels in a function-vector-like object is a follow-up for the from-scratch models.

**Pretrained LLMs and structure defined in context.** Several studies probe whether pretrained models can adopt semantics that exist only in the prompt. Overriding flipped labels, and learning from semantically unrelated labels, emerge with scale [14]. Performance on counterfactual task variants, such as arithmetic in unfamiliar bases, degrades consistently relative to the default task [15]. On randomly sampled formal languages, LLMs underperform simple n-gram baselines [16]. Analyses across the Pythia suite place ICL between memorisation and an independent symbolic algorithm [17]. Park et al. [18] show that Llama-3.1-8B (and smaller Llama and Gemma models) reorganise their representations to match a graph defined by random walks over ordinary words. However, models often cannot *use* such in-context representations downstream: Gemma-3 (4–27B) and OLMo-2-13B reach about 40% on a 4×4-grid adaptive world-modelling task, and frontier reasoning models degrade on 2-D topologies [19]. Most relevant to compounding, Matsutani et al. [20] find that few-shot demonstrations from *different worlds* (the same or a different graph topology, with words reassigned to nodes) improve in-context world modelling in Qwen3-4B/8B/14B-Base and other families, with gains that saturate as shots increase. Their demonstrations reuse one vocabulary across worlds, and we found no length-matched control or idealised baseline in the paper [our reading of the HTML; verify]. Hosseini et al. [21] argue that ICL is not one process.

**Usable information and the notebook-versus-abstraction question.** Shannon information cannot separate a learner that stores past tables verbatim (a notebook) from one that has extracted their axioms: both have the same mutual information with the target environment. Xu et al.'s predictive V-information [22] measures the information *usable* by a computationally bounded observer, and it can be created by computation, in violation of the data-processing inequality. This is the right formal frame for our equal-budget comparison. N_iso and A enumerate at most the same number of candidate tables. They differ in what the history makes cheaply usable, and that difference is visible as a gap on G-cells of held-out isomorphism types.

**Critical sets, defining sets and teaching dimension.** A critical set of an n×n Latin square is a minimal set of entries that identifies it among all Latin squares. Hatami and Qian [23] prove a quadratic lower bound towards the conjectured ⌊n²/4⌋ smallest critical set, and note that the smallest critical set is the minimum teaching dimension of the class of Latin squares. Our minimum defining sets are the analogue for the class of labelled group tables. We computed them exactly for orders up to 9 and proved a general lower bound of ⌈(n−1)/3⌉. They make cue size a principled design variable, distinct from the random-cue "snap point".

**Reconstructing group tables from queries.** Balister, Illingworth, Scott, Stein and Tamitegama study how many queries to the multiplication table of a group with unknown element order are needed for reconstruction [24]. For most abelian groups, random queries suffice with high probability once at least n−1 elements have been seen, typically after about (n/3)·log n queries. Adaptive deterministic queries need (1+o(1))n for many groups, including non-abelian ones. The n−1 threshold matches our lower-bound argument: any defining cue must mention at least n−1 distinct labels. Their question (random or adaptive queries, asymptotic) and ours (smallest fixed cue, exact small orders, relative to the class of group tables) are complementary. **Global-workspace interpretability.** Gurnee et al. [25, 26] introduce the Jacobian lens. For each vocabulary token it finds the direction that encodes the model's potential to verbalise that token later. The resulting J-space has workspace-like properties in Claude models: its contents are reportable and controllable, carry intermediate steps of silent reasoning, can be passed to arbitrary downstream computations, and are bypassed by automatic processing. Follow-ups apply the lens to open-weight models [27, 28]. This gives a concrete mechanistic version of our behavioural question. If a model compounds by abstraction, the induced axioms (for example "this operation has an identity" or "this operation is associative") might appear as workspace content reused across tables. Exemplar matching should not need the workspace. We have not tested this; it is a possible extension.
